## Key contributions

- **One specification, many submissions.** Each of 689 kernels is specified once in NumPy; the framework derives C and Fortran references and accepts submissions through a common C ABI or a Python interface, on CPUs or GPUs, on one node or several. Tracks: *loop-level reasoning* (descended from the TSVC-2 vectorizing-compiler test suite, "modernized and extended with adversarial variants"), *scientific computing* (production codes from weather and climate, electronic structure, plasma and fusion, seismology, molecular dynamics, plus proxy applications, organized by the thirteen Berkeley dwarfs), and *machine learning* (primitives to full networks, "mainly adapted from KernelBench").
- **External judge.** Correctness is graded on fuzzed inputs from two secret seeds: `/score` uses one seed during the episode and `/submit` the other, so an agent cannot tune to inputs it has seen. The judge holds the clock and a result cache hidden from agents; containers are fixed and isolated; agents "cannot hard-code answers or report their own times." Correctness is all-or-nothing (exact for integers and booleans; `|x − x_ref| ≤ atol + rtol·|x_ref|` with per-precision constants for floats, `atol` raised for reordered sums). A speedup counts only if a one-sided Mann–Whitney U test at α = 0.1 confirms it; otherwise it is credited as 1, and a confirmed slowdown is credited below 1.
- **Two reporting metrics.** *Intervention efficacy* compares paired before/after runs on the same kernels through three ratios — success ρ_R, speedup ρ_S (geometric mean over kernels solved by both), and cost ρ_C — each equal to 1 for no effect. *Weighted token cost* reports input, cached-input, and output tokens under explicitly stated weights, because "token weighting decides whether a saving is significant."
- **Submission modes.** Open, single-submission (recommended for comparing optimizers), and blind.

## Summary

The benchmark is the measurement instrument; the paper's experiments are a demonstration of what it can separate. The experiment matrix (Table II) runs three open-weight models — Qwen3.8-27B, Kimi-K2.7, and OSS-120B — over six setups: 40 focused loop-level kernels in C, Fortran, HIP, Triton, and OpenMP target offloading (12M tokens, 4 h), the same 40 blind, 10 and 35 scientific-computing kernels in C (60M tokens, 20 h), a 20-kernel harness study (14 scientific, 6 loop-level), and 19 distributed machine-learning models in HIP with RCCL on 1–16 GPUs across four nodes (24M tokens, 6 h). The primary platform is AMD MI300A (repository: "On Beverin (AMD MI300A)"); Appendix C-B names GH200 as the second platform. Baselines are typed as sequential, library-optimized, or expert-written; the default scientific-computing baseline is the fastest of C with automatic parallelization, plain C, and Numba.

## Key takeaways

- **[abstract]** "Agents reach up to 4.2× speedup over automatic parallelizers." This is a maximum, not a central value; the track, model, and geometric mean were not read.
- **[abstract]** Transfer: "93–99% of answers without device-specific code remain correct, and per-kernel speedups keep a rank correlation of r_s ≥ 0.84" on a second machine.
- **[abstract]** "Across 40 matched setups, interventions significantly change speedup only twice but token cost by up to 3.0×." The introduction says the evaluation covers "eight interventions, three open-weight models, two devices and a second machine." The conclusion repeats it: "Of 40 such comparisons, only two change speedup significantly." §III defines an intervention as adding or removing "a skill, a tool or a new prompt framing." §IV-D reports a significant speedup change for the language skill packet on OSS-120B in C (ρ_S = 0.90, a slowdown), and that withdrawing the scoring tool (blind mode) lowers speedup significantly in three of eight setups (ρ_S 0.65–0.74, for example Kimi-K2.7 in C at 0.66). The full v1 text does not itemize the 40 comparisons; since Table XII lists blind mode among the eight interventions, the three significant blind-mode drops plus the language-skill result appear to exceed "twice" unless the count uses a different significance rule (the text mentions Benjamini–Hochberg correction at q < 0.05 per panel). The "twice" count is therefore unreconciled in the source itself, not merely unread. Token weights: billed (1, 0.1, 1) for input, cached input, and output by default; effective (1, 0, 1); total (1, 1, 1). The 3.0× is the OSS-120B mini-SWE-agent saving in billed tokens, and the authors note that the weighting alone can change a cost effect up to threefold.
- **Method facts (read):** an episode stopped by its token or time limit "is rerun once with 2–4× the limit"; scaling efficiency is anchored at the baseline, not at the answer's own single-processor run, "since slow code scales well." Budgets differ across setups (4–20 h, 12M–60M tokens), so cross-setup comparisons are not matched; within-setup intervention pairs are.
- **Repository (read):** per-track automatic baselines (Numba for loop-level, NumPy for machine learning, fastest of C-autopar/C/Numba for scientific computing); `-ffast-math` is off "so results match NumPy"; an explicit compiler-flag matrix with "no literal -O3 anywhere"; framework adapters for DaCe, Numba, TVM, Triton, and JAX; internet access is off by policy during runs. The README's own count is "650 kernels," against the paper's 689.

## Why it matters for this survey

The guide has asked since the TIRx Harness and KCoral digests for an experiment that holds the model and workload fixed and varies one harness component at a time. HPCAgent-Bench is that instrument. Its authors report that added tools and skills rarely change accepted speedup significantly while changing cost by up to 3.0×; that is a statement about significance at the tested sample sizes, not evidence that the effects are zero, and the blind-mode result shows that removing feedback does lower speedup. Two design consequences follow regardless of how that count is reconciled. First, "intervention efficacy" with paired runs and a significance test should become the reporting standard for harness and skill claims in this register, replacing single-run before/after speedups. Second, the explicit token-weight disclosure addresses the search-cost reporting gap directly: a cached-input-heavy agent can look cheap or expensive depending on weights that papers rarely state. The benchmark's baselines are automatic parallelizers and library calls, not the strong structured-search arms this guide names for the first thesis-test row (EqiForge, MPK, DITRON), so "4.2× over automatic parallelizers" compares agents with the default automatic tool for loop-level and scientific code. It does not compare them with an autotuner or polyhedral optimizer on the same loops, and it says nothing about the best non-agent optimizer for tensor programs, a different task class. Read with [MetaKernelBench](metakernelbench.md) (skill transfer with negative cases) and [D2K-Bench](d2k-bench.md) (expert-guidance intervention under one harness), which measure single interventions without a general framework. Lineage: same ETH SPCL group as NPBench, whose components the repository reuses.

## Limits / caveats

- The full v1 text has been read, but individual tables and claims have not all been checked one by one: the 4.2× and 93–99% numbers are recorded as stated in the abstract, and the track composition (Table V), per-model tables, and GH200 transfer table have not been verified figure by figure. v1 does not itemize the 40 comparisons (see above).
- Open-weight models only (Qwen3.8-27B, Kimi-K2.7, OSS-120B) in the paper's experiments; frontier closed models are not in the matrix read here, so the "interventions rarely help" finding may not transfer to stronger agents.
- The speedup denominator is the benchmark's typed baseline, mostly sequential or auto-parallelized CPU code; GPU tracks use HIP, Triton, and OpenMP offload with library-optimized baselines where available, but no comparison to vendor libraries or to `torch.compile` was read.
- The repository's reproducibility caveat is unusually candid: "a rebuild from the same Dockerfile is a different image that merely resembles it," and ROCm wheels are "untested outside the MI300A images."
- Experiment that would settle more: rerun the eight-intervention matrix with a frontier model and add a structured-search arm (an autotuner or polyhedral optimizer such as Pluto, which the paper names as an optimizer option) at matched wall-clock budget, reporting ρ_S with its confidence interval alongside weighted cost.
