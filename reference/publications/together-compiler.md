## Key contributions

Together Compiler (TKCC) continuously searches executable **whole-model forward-pass implementations**, not merely standalone kernels. Agents edit the inference engine's source and kernel implementations and benchmark those changes. A **persistent hot-reload engine** tests variants in a fast high-throughput tier and a slower, more representative tier. An analytical **non-agent** cost model ranks tensor/expert/pipeline/data parallelism choices before expensive search. The compiler/runtime still implements execution, cost modeling, compilation, measurement, correctness and deployment checks. The agent is a proposer; the paper's co-designed measurement and cost infrastructure is a substantial source of value.

The authors report production serving of trillions of tokens across their fleet. That is a **first-party deployment assertion**, not an independently inspected production promotion log. The new object optimized is a forward-pass implementation, not a reusable generated compiler analysis, transformation or lowering installed across subsequent toolchain releases.

## Summary

Together AI built and deployed a continual compilation loop for its inference service. Agents edit inference-engine and kernel source, a persistent hot-reload engine measures candidates in two fidelity tiers, gates check correctness, and an analytical cost model picks parallelism. Demonstrated: application-level throughput gains over the best published InferenceX engine configurations on six model/hardware settings, under a 24-hour, two-node cap per setting, with a published workload protocol and some failure counts. Claimed but not independently inspectable: that the system "continually compiles our production fleet" and serves trillions of tokens per day. Not demonstrated: that agent reasoning beats a matched non-agent optimizer on the same action space, or that any generated component is reused across compiler releases.

## Key takeaways: author-reported results and denominators

- **Application throughput:** Up to **2.6× higher tokens/s** than the best published open-source InferenceX engine configurations as of **27 October 2026**, across **Kimi K3, GLM 5.3, MiniMax M3.1 Flash and DeepSeek v4.1 Flash** and multiple accelerator settings. This is an **up-to** result, not an across-model mean or isolated model effect; the paper does not give a matched non-agent search controller on the same engine/action space.
- **Workhorse model:** Up to **1.7× TPS** versus hand-optimized persistent megakernels on **Llama 1B, batch size 1**, with H100/B200 runs and varying output lengths. That comparison has a different baseline and task denominator from the InferenceX result.
- **End-to-end protocol:** The NVIDIA and AMD InferenceX AgentX workload uses **393 public agentic-coding traces (seed 42)**, pinned AIPerf **1.0.12**, and throughput per GPU counting cached input tokens in full plus output tokens over wall-clock time; P50/P90 inter-token latency is separately evaluated. The authors reran the published engine configurations and report reproducing baseline throughput, with approximately **2%** P90 interactivity discrepancy. Their Figure 7 starts with the *same parallelism strategy as the leading configuration* to reduce that confound. **Trainium2** is assessed on a different synthetic multi-turn dataset (Appendix A.3, PDF p.22: **65,792-token context** with a 3,456-token shared system prompt, about 95% cache hit rate, exactly 700 output tokens) and must not be treated as a directly identical AgentX workload.
- **Optimization budget:** **24 continuous hours per model/configuration**, with **two times the engine replica size**, one for each tier; the headline says **at most two nodes per model**, not a universal fixed GPU-hour cost. Report amortized development and production infrastructure costs separately.
- **Search-system throughput:** The persistent engine admits **252× more experiments** and **11× lower agent idle time** than a restart-per-experiment RSI-Inference comparator. That is a controlled **measurement-system** comparison, *not* 252× kernel throughput, 252× application TPS, or agent superiority over non-agent search.
- **Reliability and measurement:** For the evaluated MiniMax on TensorRT-LLM setting, Table 1 gives startup-to-first-job 11 vs 3 minutes, hot reload 45 vs 17 seconds, median job completion 64 vs 28 seconds (slow vs fast), and fast-tier **3.9 crashes per 1,000 jobs** vs zero in the slow tier. The latter is a particular tier's job-crash rate, **not** the optimization campaign's total invalid-candidate/failure/rollback rate. **Additional failure evidence:** Appendix C.3 explicitly reports **9** illegal-memory-access GPU resets (Xid 154) during GLM 5.3 and **2** machines removed from the cluster; it also says a closed model disabled editing some GPU execution steps after crashes, removing some possible optimizations. These are first-party infrastructure/safety failures, not a complete denominator. The count is read from PDF v1 p.32 (9 resets, 2 machines); an earlier reviewer transcription of 99/22 was an error. Fast-tier approximations require a higher-fidelity gate.
- **Candidate throughput and uncertainty:** the fast+slow tiers assessed **1,354 candidates** versus **168** with a single slow tier (8.1×) and found a solution reported 7.2% faster; this is a harness-configuration ablation, not a non-agent search arm. The paper reports back-to-back engine variance of **4–6%**, reducing from **6.6% to 2.7%** after splitting prefill/decode measurement. The end-to-end Figure 7 has **six throughput–interactivity curves** for different model/hardware settings and no machine-readable per-setting ratio table, confidence intervals or common median; it cannot justify a new six-setting median or reproducibility claim without careful extraction and reruns.
- **Figure 8, separate low-concurrency cost-model comparison (not Figure 7's fixed-parallelism series):** each pair is published baseline TPS/user → TKCC TPS/user (P90): MiniMax M3.1 B200 **633→1111** (1.755×), DeepSeek v4.1 B200 **671→813** (1.212×), GLM 5.3 B200 **329→417** (1.267×), Kimi K3 GB300 **423→450** (1.064×), DeepSeek v4.1 GB300 **943→1205** (1.278×), and GLM 5.3 MI355X **463→480** (1.037×). The six illustrative ratios have an arithmetic-sample median **≈1.24×**, but **this is not the Figure 7 median or a statistically validated cohort result**. For MiniMax, the cost model moved the configuration from TP4 to TP8. The authors report that the unchanged InferenceX image at TP8 performs *similarly* to the published TP4 result (§5.2), so most of the 633→1,111 gain comes from TKCC's optimization loop at TP8, not from the parallelism switch. That loop combines agent-written edits with the persistent engine and its gates; the paper does not separate them, and the TP8 baseline is described only as "similar", not quantified. 1.755× strongly exceeds the reported 4–6% short-run spread under that qualitative same-image TP8 check; it is not a statistically reproduced fixed-TP8 causal effect. Kimi K3/GB300 (1.064×) and GLM 5.3/MI355X (1.037×) do not clearly separate from the 4–6% spread. The six ratios are not independent replicas.
- **Context ablation:** After 12 hours, a curated knowledge hub yields **25–31% less speedup** than no knowledge hub on the authors' assessed runs; simple common optimization instructions beat their specialized expert strategies. These are specific agent-harness context comparisons; they do not prove persistent compiler memory or knowledge-guided search generically harmful.

## Causal interpretation and strong non-agent alternatives

The paper is **strong first-party application-level evidence that a deployed agent-assisted compilation workflow can optimize serving systems**, not a causal demonstration that agent reasoning outperforms a matched conventional optimizer. Gains combine agent-authored source changes, the analytic parallelism cost model, persistent high-fidelity measurement, two-tier promotion, and access to whole-engine host metadata. Improvement over a baseline compiled in another engine is not an ablation of each piece.

For the **2027 predicate**, the strong published conventional inference engines in InferenceX are plausible **strong non-agent baselines** under the rule as written: a matched conventional *search-policy* arm is **not** an extra admission requirement. The separate **§5.3 agent-advantage hypothesis** does demand such a policy comparison. Qualifying the 2027 result still requires an attributable agent-derived application improvement exceeding measurement uncertainty, with a disclosed budget, validation, and meaningful failure record. The named Kimi K3 production gain (up to 24% TPS / 18% TPM, §4.3) is credited to the non-agent cost model and must not be reclassified as an agent-written-code outcome. The MiniMax TP8 gain, by contrast, is attributable to the optimization loop (agent edits plus measurement and gates), per the authors' same-image TP8 check.

A strong falsifier tests **the same persistent hot-reload engine and two-tier gate**, with the **same edits, compiler checks, knowledge, traffic, and 24-hour × two-replica budget**, using (a) the paper's agent, (b) EqiForge/TASO/beam/evolutionary/bandit-style non-agent optimization adapted to that action API, (c) fixed conventional tuning, and (d) agent variants with and without curated memory. Report all executed/correct/incorrect trials, accepted p50/p90 serving TPS, total accelerator-hours and tokens, regressions, promotions/rollbacks and retention on held-out traffic and hardware. The non-agent systems are **comparison designs**, not previously measured TKCC results.

### Proposed experiment for §5.3

Freeze one model, precision, parallelism, hardware allocation, starting implementation and request trace. Expose the same legal kernel and host-dispatch edits, cost model, profiles and correctness diagnostics to all arms. Compare a version-pinned agent proposer, an adaptive evolutionary search over those edits, and a fixed expert tuning schedule. Select conventional settings on development traffic before sealing the test trace. If an agent discovers an edit outside the shared interface, record it separately, expose it to all three arms, and rerun the comparison. To isolate use of feedback within the agent, compare against the same model and token allowance with static workload information only.

**Policy comparison.** Run independent searches in paired hardware and traffic blocks, rotate arm order and isolate caches. Use identical promotion rules and count invalid, duplicate and timed-out proposals against the cap. Compare the best validated candidate at a common proposal count and at a common total-cost ceiling. Include model calls, compilation, accelerator allocation, idle time and recovery. One pilot configuration is 128 proposals with at most one full serving test per eight proposals. Calibrate budgets and replication on independent pilot data and freeze them before the formal runs.

**Evaluator comparison.** Freeze a stratified manifest across arms, retaining slow, failed and fast-tier-rejected candidates. Replay the same candidates under four configurations: restart or persistent hot reload, each crossed with full measurement or fast screening followed by full measurement. Counterbalance replay order, and measure every runnable candidate separately in a clean engine. Report candidates evaluated per accelerator-hour, the fraction of true baseline-beating candidates discarded, and the clean-engine throughput of each selected winner. This replay estimates evaluator effects conditional on the manifest. A policy-by-evaluator interaction requires rerunning the adaptive searches under each evaluator.

**Acceptance.** Lock each winner before measuring held-out traffic. Application throughput is completed output tokens divided by elapsed time from first arrival through last completion or timeout. Fix arrivals, output-length rules and timeouts. Report request-level P90 end-to-end latency including queue time, counting timeouts at their deadline, alongside failed requests per arrival. Report correct candidates per executed candidate, held-out numerical and quality checks, invalid proposals per submission, compile/runtime failures per attempted trial, resets, removals and rollbacks. A search yielding no accepted improvement returns the starting implementation. Report both cost to first accepted improvement and total cost.

As illustrative preregistration values, repeat ten independent paired searches and remeasure each winner three times in fresh engines. Use independent search pairs as the uncertainty unit. For example, declare an agent advantage over adaptive search only if the lower 95% paired confidence bound for their equal-cost throughput ratio exceeds 1.05, all required correctness checks pass, and prespecified latency and request-success limits hold (for example, at most 2% P90 degradation). An upper bound below 1.05 rules out that target effect for this workload and budget; an interval spanning the threshold leaves the comparison open. These values are proposed design choices, not published measurements.

**Public implementation.** Use an open-weight model in vLLM and a profiled Triton kernel with shared kernel variants and host dispatch choices. Replay fixed shapes in the fast tier and requests spanning prefill and decode in the serving tier. A resident worker switching precompiled variants tests that specific switching mechanism. The result applies to this action interface, traffic and hardware; Together's unrestricted source edits, internal engine and production gain require separate validation. A harness needs swappable proposers and evaluator-owned gates, budgets and replay logs for this comparison. A foundation needs replayable, versioned action semantics; extend the representation when a valuable omitted edit survives a matched rerun.

## Why it matters for this survey

This is the first source in the survey where an agent-driven optimization loop is integrated into a shipped serving path and evaluated at the application level against strong published non-agent engines. It informs three design choices. First, the action space: agents edit host-side engine source that a graph intermediate representation cannot express, which is the opposite of the typed, extensible intermediate representation path documented by [TIRx](tirx.md). Second, the evaluator: the persistent two-tier engine (252× more experiments than restart-per-experiment) suggests that measurement infrastructure, not agent reasoning, may be the binding constraint on search. Third, supplied knowledge: the curated hub underperformed, which bears on the controller checkpoint's supplied-knowledge arm alongside [AKLR](aklr.md) and [MetaKernelBench](metakernelbench.md). It is one organization's first-party evaluation; the predecessor compilers and persistent megakernels the paper builds on share its lineage and are not independent confirmation.

## Agent / harness / compiler architectural signal

- **Problem:** traditional intermediate graphs cannot express all host-side changes relevant to data-dependent mixture-of-experts or long-sequence attention; engine restart makes high-fidelity iteration expensive.
- **Mechanism:** let the proposer inspect and rewrite source-level forward passes, with the compiler, analytics and execution harness retaining measurement, validation, cost modeling and selected promotion gates.
- **What evolves:** per-model implementations and strategy via repeated searches. A persistent versioned compiler-controller policy or reusable compiler pass is **not** shown to self-evolve by a held-out comparison.
- **Protected boundary:** high-fidelity on-traffic evaluation and numerical checks; the paper's published failure record does not establish exhaustive independent certification or automated rollback safety.
- **Design alternative:** a typed extensible intermediate representation such as TIRx might add checkability, but the two projects have not compared attainable application performance or full engineering cost.
